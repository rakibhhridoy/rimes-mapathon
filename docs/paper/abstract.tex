Flood susceptibility maps are judged by how well they rank the ground that floods, yet they are rarely compared with the simplest alternative, the record of where floods have already been. This study makes that comparison for infrastructure mapped in OpenStreetMap in three regions of Bangladesh, with flood extents mapped from Sentinel-1 radar for thirteen events as labels and reference and every model scored on 10\,km blocks held out from training. Trained on floods up to 2022, a gradient-boosted model ranked the ground the 2024 river floods reached in Rangpur and Rajshahi at an AUC of \RRTempAUC{}, below the record of earlier flooding at \RRTempPastAUC{}, and the record kept its lead with masks re-mapped from a single satellite, with two other training targets and with 40\,km blocks. Given the record as a feature, the model beat both, at \RRPastFeatAUC{}, by ranking the ground no earlier flood had reached. In the Sylhet haor the record's lead, \SylhetTempPastAUC{} against \SylhetTempAUC{}, closed when the model learned the same target as the test, and on the cyclone-struck coast, where most flooded assets stood on ground no earlier cyclone had reached, the model's lead did not survive wider blocks. The record was therefore the stronger guide where floods returned to the same ground. Radar-observed extents also made better training labels than terrain thresholds, by \RRLabelGap{}, \SylhetLabelGap{} and \CoastalLabelGap{} in AUC on held-out areas, a graph neural network added nothing over tabular models, and elevation carried almost all of the terrain signal. The radar masks agreed only moderately with an independent MODIS map, so every score measures agreement with flooding as radar sees it. Susceptibility studies with a radar flood history should report that record as a baseline.
