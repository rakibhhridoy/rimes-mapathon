\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Riverine and flash floods recur across northern Bangladesh in most monsoon seasons and cyclone surges strike its south-west coast, yet the susceptibility maps meant to show where flooding would do most harm are rarely tested against floods that actually occurred. The studies closest to this one report best-model AUCs between 0.87 and 0.98 \citep{islam2021, talukdar2020, islamr2024, adnan2023}, and two choices decide what such a figure means. The first is where the flood locations come from, usually a few hundred to a few thousand points drawn from field surveys, local knowledge or satellite flood maps. The second is how the test set is separated from the training set, since a random split of spatially clustered points tests a model mostly beside its own training points, a design known to inflate accuracy \citep{roberts2017, ploton2020}.

Both choices can now be made differently. Sentinel-1 radar sees through monsoon cloud and has been processed into flood maps with overall accuracies above 94\,\% \citep{twele2016, uddin2019}. Every pixel observed to flood can therefore serve as a training label \citep{islamr2024}, and whole areas can be held out so that the test asks how well a model carries to ground it has not seen. A radar record of several floods also offers a baseline that susceptibility studies rarely report, which is the map of where water has already been. Graph neural networks, which pass information between neighbouring locations, have meanwhile entered hazard mapping \citep{wang2023, ma2025} on the appeal that a road or embankment floods together with its neighbours, although whether that structure adds anything over an ordinary tabular model has seldom been tested on identical data.

This study asks three questions of flood susceptibility models for mapped infrastructure in three regions with contrasting regimes, riverine flooding in Rangpur and Rajshahi, flash flooding in the Sylhet haor basin and cyclone surge on the south-west coast. The first, and the most consequential for how published maps should be read, is whether radar-observed flood extents make better training labels than terrain thresholds for ground a model has not seen. The second is whether a model anticipates a later flood better than the record of earlier ones, and the third is whether a graph neural network outperforms ordinary tabular models given the same features and blocks. The flood hazard is then combined with exposure and vulnerability on a 500\,m grid, and the maps, data and code are released openly.

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Flood susceptibility in Bangladesh}

Table~\ref{tab:litflood} summarises the studies closest to this one, each checked against its full text. They report high accuracies, from 0.81--0.87 for five models in the Teesta basin \citep{islam2021} to 0.984 for a random forest on the south-west coast \citep{adnan2023}, but every study whose split could be read divided its points at random, and none holds out whole areas. The inventories also differ in kind, from points identified by field survey and local residents \citep{islam2021, talukdar2020} or a water-board survey \citep{rudra2023} to pixels sampled from satellite flood maps \citep{islamr2024, adnan2023}, so the same accuracy figure can measure agreement with quite different things. Two studies compared the maps their models produced, and those comparisons are the more instructive. On the south-west coast four models each classified roughly 60\,\% of the land as flood-prone, yet their pixel-wise correlations ranged from 0.62 to 0.91 \citep{adnan2023}. In one Sylhet sub-district a random forest and a support vector machine with AUCs of 0.964 and 0.954 agreed on only 73.3\,\% of the map \citep{islamr2024}. Models that score equally well can therefore disagree about where flooding will happen, which is the property a map exists to convey. Multi-criteria hazard maps built from similar terrain factors, including TWI and HAND, reached validation scores of 0.77--0.79 in the Sangu basin and placed 39\,\% of roads and 25\,\% of education buildings in the highest hazard class \citep{zzaman2021}, which shows why the exposure of infrastructure matters to planners.

\subsection{Labels and validation}

Radar offers training labels that cover every pixel. Sentinel-1 flood maps have reached overall accuracies of 94.0--96.1\,\% \citep{twele2016} and 96.44\,\% for the 2017 Bangladesh floods \citep{uddin2019}, and daily MODIS imagery has mapped 913 large floods worldwide \citep{tellman2021}, although radar still struggles in vegetated and urban areas \citep{amitrano2024}, and Sentinel-1 flood maps have already been used to estimate flood exposure across a whole country \citep{sherpa2022}. One Bangladesh study has already built its inventory from Sentinel-1, for a single Sylhet flood and with a random split \citep{islamr2024}. Where no inventory exists, labels are often thresholded from the topographic wetness index \citep{beven1979} and height above nearest drainage \citep{nobre2011}, the same indices that serve as predictors, so a model scored against such labels can succeed by relearning the rule that made them.

Random cross-validation of spatially structured data underestimates prediction error, and splitting by area can address this \citep{roberts2017}. The effect can be large, since a forest-biomass model that explained more than half the variance under random validation showed almost no skill under spatial validation \citep{ploton2020}. Blocking can force a model to extrapolate \citep{roberts2017} and has no theoretical basis for estimating map accuracy \citep{wadoux2021}, so blocked scores are read here as performance on unseen areas of similar terrain, not as design-based map accuracy. Calibration is treated apart from ranking, by isotonic regression \citep{zadrozny2002, niculescu2005}, and none of the Bangladesh studies read in full reports calibrated probabilities.

\subsection{Graphs, interpolation and composite risk}

Comparisons of many learning models are common in susceptibility mapping, and among 14 models for flood-prone areas of Pakistan boosted trees ranked first \citep{waleed2025}. Graph neural networks learn from a node's neighbours and, in the inductive form used here, generalise to nodes unseen in training \citep{hamilton2017}. In hazard mapping they have appeared mainly for landslides \citep{wang2023, ma2025}, a review of 58 deep-learning flood studies found convolutional models usually the more accurate \citep{bentivoglio2022}, and the searches made for this review found no graph model of the flood susceptibility of mapped infrastructure. Composite indices such as INFORM combine hazard, exposure and vulnerability by geometric mean \citep{inform2017}, and the same aggregation is used here, with exposure counted asset by asset as \citet{koks2019} did globally. That exposure depends on OpenStreetMap, whose building completeness exceeds 80\,\% in some cities but falls below 20\,\% in cities holding 48\,\% of the world's urban population \citep{herfort2023}, and whose completeness across rural Bangladesh has not been measured.

\section{Study area and data}

Three regions with contrasting flood regimes are studied, riverine flooding in Rangpur and Rajshahi, flash flooding in the Sylhet haor basin and cyclone surge on the south-west coast (Fig.~\ref{fig:study}a, Table~\ref{tab:regions}), and they share one set of model settings so that results stay comparable. Every dataset the pipeline ingests is openly licensed (Table~\ref{tab:data}). Administrative boundaries come from geoBoundaries, which unlike GADM may be redistributed and carries the union level for Bangladesh. OpenStreetMap coverage is uneven, so an area with few mapped assets shows low exposure whether or not much stands there, and WorldPop's constrained product carries population only where buildings were detected in imagery.

\subsection{Assets, terrain and features}

Assets are fetched from OpenStreetMap in three tag groups, lifelines (hospitals, schools, shelters, bridges, embankments), transport (primary to trunk roads, railways) and agriculture (farmland, aquaculture, canals, markets), and each is classified into one asset type. From the 30\,m SRTM elevation model, reprojected to UTM zone 46N, the pipeline derives slope, the topographic wetness index (TWI), height above nearest drainage (HAND) and D8 flow accumulation. Each asset carries these five terrain values at its representative point, WorldPop population density, its distance to the nearest hospital, school, flood shelter, road and irrigation channel, and its asset type, all standardised, and the pipeline stops if any feature is constant or the labels hold a single class.

\subsection{Flood extents from Sentinel-1}
\label{sec:observed}

Flood extents mapped from Sentinel-1 radar supply both the training labels and the reference against which the models are scored. Following the change-detection method recommended by UN-SPIDER \citep{unspider}, an approach that outperforms single-image thresholding for Sentinel-1 \citep{dadhich2026}, the minimum VV backscatter in each flood window is compared in Google Earth Engine with the median of a dry-season baseline from the same year. A pixel is flagged as flooded where it is below $-16$\,dB and at least 3\,dB darker than its baseline. Permanent water from the JRC record \citep{pekel2016}, slopes above 5$^\circ$ and specks under eight pixels are removed, and the event masks are averaged into a flood-frequency layer (Fig.~\ref{fig:study}b--d). Five events are mapped in Rangpur and Rajshahi, four in Sylhet and four cyclone landfalls on the coast, each window set from ReliefWeb situation reports (Table~\ref{tab:events}).

The masks were checked against an independent map of two of the same floods, the August 2017 flood in Rangpur and Rajshahi and the April 2017 haor flood in Sylhet, which the Global Flood Database maps from MODIS at 250\,m \citep{tellman2021}. On that grid, over cells that MODIS saw clear and that are not permanent water, the maps agree on \RRXcAgree{}\,\% and \SylhetXcAgree{}\,\% of cells, a figure dominated by dry ground. On flooded ground the agreement is only moderate, with a critical success index of \RRXcCSI{} and \SylhetXcCSI{} and a kappa of \RRXcKappa{} and \SylhetXcKappa{}. The radar marks more ground as flooded than MODIS in Rangpur and Rajshahi (\RRXcSOneShare{}\,\% against \RRXcGfdShare{}\,\%), consistent with flooded patches smaller than a MODIS pixel and with flooding hidden by cloud. It marks less in the Sylhet haor (\SylhetXcSOneShare{}\,\% against \SylhetXcGfdShare{}\,\%), consistent with the difficulty radar has where water stands under vegetation \citep{amitrano2024}, as it did under unharvested rice in April 2017. Neither explanation was tested, and neither map is ground truth.

\subsection{Training labels}
\label{sec:labels}

In the riverine regions ground counts as flood-prone where it flooded in at least two of the mapped events, while on the coast one cyclone window is enough, because surge drains within days and the six-to-twelve-day revisit leaves too few pixels flooded twice. The pipeline also writes a terrain-threshold ensemble, which marks a pixel as flood-prone where at least two of three indicators agree, TWI above 8, HAND below 5\,m and JRC water occurrence above 25\,\%. TWI and HAND are also model inputs, so a model trained and scored on these labels can succeed by relearning the threshold rule.

\subsection{Asset model}
\label{sec:gnn}

The default asset model is class-weighted gradient boosting with 300 iterations and a learning rate of 0.1, chosen by the comparison in Section~\ref{sec:benchmark}, where it is set against a two-layer GraphSAGE network \citep{hamilton2017} on a six-nearest-neighbour graph of assets, a random forest and logistic regression. Validation holds out whole 10\,km blocks, assigned at random to training (80\,\%), calibration and test, because a random split leaves every test asset beside training assets with almost the same features and label. Scores are calibrated by isotonic regression on the calibration blocks, with each level shrunk towards the middle when it holds few points, and every metric comes from the test blocks. In the two riverine regions the published map scores come from the same model given one more feature, the share of earlier events in which the ground flooded (Section~\ref{sec:temporal}). Every validation figure outside that section comes from the model without it, because the feature is built from the events the models are scored against.

\subsection{Hazard surfaces}
\label{sec:hazard}

Two hazard surfaces cover the grid. The first interpolates the asset scores by ordinary Kriging, which can add little between assets where the scores lack spatial structure, and the fitted variograms leave \RRNuggetSillRatio{} of the sill unexplained at zero distance in Rangpur and Rajshahi and \SylhetNuggetSillRatio{} in Sylhet. The second, the default, is a class-weighted logistic regression on each cell's own elevation, slope, TWI, HAND and flow accumulation, trained at the assets on the observed labels and blocks and evaluated at every 30\,m cell. It leaves out the access distances, which describe exposure rather than the likelihood of flooding, and so follows individual channels and levees where the Kriged surface varies over tens of kilometres (Supporting Information, Fig.~S1).

\subsection{Exposure, vulnerability and composite risk}
\label{sec:aggregation}

Exposure is measured twice for each 500\,m cell, once as the type-weighted count of mapped assets and once as WorldPop density, both log-scaled. Vulnerability combines population density with the distance to the nearest hospital, flood shelter and primary road, and components without data are left out rather than filled with a constant. Composite risk is the geometric mean of hazard, exposure and vulnerability, as in INFORM \citep{inform2017}, so that no exposure means no risk, and cells are placed in five relative classes by quintile. Cells and administrative units with no mapped assets carry no score, since sparse mapping would otherwise read as low risk. Results are summarised for every union, upazila and district (Fig.~\ref{fig:unionrisk}), and the weights, the hotspot method and a sensitivity analysis are in the Supporting Information (Sections~S2 and S3). The ranking of cells is robust to the chosen weights, with a mean Spearman correlation of \RRSensRandomRho{} with the published ranking when every weight is perturbed at random in Rangpur and Rajshahi, and it changes materially only when vulnerability is reduced to population density alone ($\rho=\RRSensPopOnlyRho{}$).

\subsection{Validation design}
\label{sec:design}

Every model is scored on the held-out blocks by the area under the ROC curve (AUC), average precision and its lift over the base rate, and the Brier score, and the hazard surfaces are also scored against observed flooding over every grid cell. Every comparison between models or label sources is repeated over \RRBenchSeeds{} block assignments drawn with different seeds and tested by a paired comparison across them, because one assignment gives a single number with no sense of its spread. A temporal test trains on the floods up to 2022 and scores against those of 2024 (Section~\ref{sec:temporal}).

\subsection{Observed extents against terrain labels}
\label{sec:labelresult}

The terrain-threshold ensemble captures \RRProxyPOD{} of the ground that flooded in at least \RRMinEvents{} of the five Rangpur and Rajshahi events, with a false alarm ratio of \RRProxyFAR{}, and marks \RRProxyShare{}\,\% of the region as flood-prone where the radar saw \RRObservedShare{}\,\% flood that often. Trained on those labels and scored on the same held-out blocks against observed flooding over \RRBenchSeeds{} assignments, the default model reaches \RRLabelProxyAUC{} against \RRLabelObsAUC{} for observed labels in Rangpur and Rajshahi, \SylhetLabelProxyAUC{} against \SylhetLabelObsAUC{} in Sylhet and \CoastalLabelProxyAUC{} against \CoastalLabelObsAUC{} on the coast. The gaps of \RRLabelGap{}, \SylhetLabelGap{} and \CoastalLabelGap{} favour the observed labels on \RRLabelAhead{}, \SylhetLabelAhead{} and \CoastalLabelAhead{} of the assignments ($p\RRLabelGapP{}$, $p\SylhetLabelGapP{}$ and $p\CoastalLabelGapP{}$; Fig.~\ref{fig:roc}). This test favours the observed labels, since that model is scored against the definition it learned, so Section~\ref{sec:temporal} repeats it against later floods that neither label source was trained on.

\subsection{The south-west coast}

On the coast the asset model still ranks flooded ground above dry ground (AUC \CoastalValAUC{}), but across the grid both hazard surfaces are close to chance, the terrain surface at \CoastalSurfaceTerrainObsAUC{} and the Kriged one at \CoastalSurfaceKrigedObsAUC{}. The same two surfaces reach \RRSurfaceTerrainObsAUC{} and \RRSurfaceKrigedObsAUC{} in Rangpur and Rajshahi and \SylhetSurfaceTerrainObsAUC{} and \SylhetSurfaceKrigedObsAUC{} in Sylhet. Surge flooding is governed by polder embankments, tidal channels and landfall position, none of which elevation describes.

The first is the share of earlier events in which the ground flooded, which is the map the radar record alone provides. \section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Labels decide what an accuracy figure means}

The label source bears most directly on published flood maps. Terrain-threshold labels cost the model \RRLabelGap{}, \SylhetLabelGap{} and \CoastalLabelGap{} in AUC on held-out blocks. Against later floods that neither label source had seen they still cost \RRTempProxyGap{}, \SylhetTempProxyGap{} and \CoastalTempProxyGap{}, so the advantage of radar labels does not depend on the test sharing their definition, although in Sylhet part of it did. The indices those labels are built on, TWI and HAND, add almost nothing to the model once elevation is known, and a model scored against labels derived from its own inputs measures its recovery of the labelling rule rather than its skill at locating floods. The mean AUCs here, \RRBenchBoostingAUC{} and \SylhetBenchBoostingAUC{} in the riverine regions, sit at or below the best-model AUCs of 0.87 to 0.98 in Table~\ref{tab:litflood}. The comparison is only indicative, since those studies split their points at random while every figure here comes from 10\,km blocks the model never saw. Blocking has its own critics \citep{roberts2017, wadoux2021}, and the blocked scores estimate performance on unseen areas of similar terrain rather than design-based map accuracy.

\subsection{What the flood record already knows}

Susceptibility studies rarely ask whether a model anticipates a later flood better than the record of earlier ones. In both riverine regions it did not, yet given that record as a feature it beat both, and it did so on ground no earlier flood had reached, where the record cannot rank at all. The record marks where water returns and the terrain ranks where it has not yet been seen, so a susceptibility map for a river basin with a radar flood history should be built on both. On the coast, where most of the ground Cyclone Remal flooded had not flooded in the earlier cyclones, the model trained on those cyclones did best. A study that reports accuracy without the past-flooding reference cannot show that its model knows more than the flood record already does, and the two are simple to compare wherever Sentinel-1 has observed earlier floods.

\subsection{What the graph did not add}

The graph network rested on the reasoning that neighbouring assets flood together, yet on identical features and blocks it trailed ordinary tabular models, measurably so in both riverine regions. A likely reason is that the features already carry the neighbourhood, since nearby assets share elevation and distances to the same roads, schools and shelters, the access distances rank second only to elevation, and a tree can split on those values directly, although this was not tested. Kriging failed in a related way, smoothing the scores over tens of kilometres while flooding follows individual channels and levees. At this scale neither linking assets into a graph nor interpolating between them improved spatial prediction, and studies proposing either should report the tabular baseline on the same blocks.

\subsection{Implications for flood risk management}
\label{sec:implications}

The results bear on flood risk management in three ways. First, where Sentinel-1 has observed several floods, the record of where water has already stood is a stronger guide to the next riverine flood than a terrain model alone, and it costs little to compute, so agencies such as the Bangladesh Water Development Board and the Flood Forecasting and Warning Centre could publish it as a planning layer beside any susceptibility map. The terrain model adds most where the record is silent, on ground no mapped flood has reached and on a coast where each surge strikes new ground. Second, a susceptibility map used to site shelters, embankments or schools should be judged on held-out areas and later floods rather than on random splits, since the accuracy a random split reports overstates how far the map can be trusted in places it has not seen. Third, the composite risk classes rank unions within a region and must be read with their gaps, because a union without mapped assets carries no score rather than a low one. The best tabular models tested here differed by less than 0.01 in AUC, so better OpenStreetMap coverage of rural unions would probably improve the map more than a new model would. Infrastructure also fails in networks, and the disruption of services beyond a flooded asset, which asset-level exposure omits, needs network modelling of the kind developed for Accra \citep{schotten2023}. Validated against losses, maps of this kind could help set priorities among the structural and non-structural measures proposed for flood-prone districts such as Sirajganj \citep{ali2019}.

\subsection{Limits of the maps}

The coast is the clearest limit. Both hazard surfaces are close to chance there, because surge flooding follows embankments, tidal channels and landfall position, which the pipeline does not yet ingest, so the coastal composite is not a map of surge exposure. Exposure depends on OpenStreetMap, whose rural completeness is unmeasured, and composite risk is defined only where assets are mapped, \RRMappedCellShare{}\,\% of grid cells in Rangpur and Rajshahi, while vulnerability omits income, housing and age structure, dimensions that a household survey in flood-prone Sunamganj found to matter \citep{rahmanm2025}. The radar labels carry error of their own, agreeing only moderately with an independent MODIS map and counting inundated paddy as flooding. Every score here therefore measures agreement with flooding mapped by radar rather than with flooding on the ground. The calibrated probabilities hold for a region as a whole rather than for any one area. The composite risk has not been checked against losses, and damage records for the 2022 and 2024 floods, where available, or depth-damage curves for infrastructure components such as those derived by expert elicitation \citep{williams2025}, would allow the risk classes to be tested against the harm they are meant to anticipate. None of the surfaces is a forecast, and warnings remain the task of the Flood Forecasting and Warning Centre and the Bangladesh Meteorological Department.

\section{Conclusions}
\label{sec:conclusions}

Radar-observed flood extents made better training labels than terrain thresholds, by \RRLabelGap{}, \SylhetLabelGap{} and \CoastalLabelGap{} in AUC on held-out ground and by \RRTempProxyGap{}, \SylhetTempProxyGap{} and \CoastalTempProxyGap{} against later floods, which bears on how any map trained and scored against terrain-derived labels should be read. In the riverine regions the record of earlier floods predicted the 2024 flooding better than the model, and a model given that record as a feature beat both, reaching \RRPastFeatAUC{} and \SylhetPastFeatAUC{}. Ordinary tabular models matched or beat a graph neural network, with elevation and the access distances carrying the signal. The terrain hazard surface followed observed flooding in the riverine regions and failed on the coast. Every number in this paper is generated from the released pipeline outputs, so each can be checked and rebuilt.
